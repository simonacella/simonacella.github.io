## Interview with Rwandan director Myriam U. Birara, whose film _The Bride_ explores post-genocide Rwanda by shining a spotlight on ‘guterura’ – the abduction of girls for the purpose of marriage, which has long been a common practice.

![The-Bride.jpg](/assets/img/posts/The-Bride.jpg)

_The Bride_, the debut feature film by Rwandan director Myriam U. Birara, is set in 1997 and centres on Eva, a young woman who dreams of becoming a doctor but is abducted and forced into a marriage with a stranger. This practice, known as ‘guterura’, was widespread in the country until recently.
Eva finds herself living with her husband’s cousin, with whom she forms a deep friendship. Her husband is often away, and the two young women spend a great deal of time together, learning to trust one another. Until the moment when Eva is forced to make a difficult decision...

With a cinematic vision that blends realism and symbolism, simplicity and depth, the director – who is also
[a painter](https://komezart.com/collections/myriam-u-birara? srsltid=AfmBOopRJqNS-vn-a04mX1O-GExgcIHCCLNyWhAPlsKzn02_WxTEcBti), is a fully-fledged member of the African Nouvelle Vague, demonstrating how it is possible to make independent, political and feminist cinema without having to rely on convoluted and sometimes ambiguous European funding.

In an interview during Open Doors 2025–2028 Focus Africa, an initiative of the Locarno Film Festival, the director spoke to us about her filmmaking practice

**How and why did you decide to make films?**

In 2009, I saw a short film by a Rwandan director, shot in Kinyarwanda, which portrayed life in the country in a realistic way. The long silences in the film weren’t just a stylistic choice but a representation of something that really existed around me. It was realistic but also beautiful and poetic. It was the opposite of the films we were watching at the time.
In 2009, most of the films being made in Rwanda, Tanzania or Nigeria depicted a lavish lifestyle that didn’t match what I saw in the country. I was still at secondary school, and when I saw this film, I realised I wanted to make films. I started looking for information on how to make a film. Until then, I’d expressed myself through drawing and painting. I’m a self-taught director.

**The Bride seems like a very simple film, but it actually tackles complex issues, such as the practice of guterura and female solidarity. Why did you choose to tell this story?**

For me, it was urgent to make this film, because I grew up in the late 1990s hearing stories of women forced into marriage. Sometimes it was neighbours, sometimes aunts; even the kidnappers were family members. You might have known him, known that he’d abducted his wife, but life seemed to carry on; she’d stayed with him, and it wasn’t clear whether she’d suffered any injuries or whether their marriage actually worked. I’ve always wondered: how did these people get away with it? And how do these women cope with the situation? Because on the surface everything seemed fine; most of the women stayed with their abductors and had children. This kept haunting me and I wanted my first feature film to be about this.
I chose to set it in 1997 because that was the last time I heard stories of abductions; after that, the practice was banned. Today in Rwanda, these abductions no longer take place, although I’ve heard that in some neighbouring countries they still happen.

**The film also deals with the reconstruction of society following the 1994 genocide. How did you tackle this theme?**

Films should be complex, just like life. That’s why I included the genocide as a backdrop. In 1997, people were still in shock; the wound was still raw. There were people who didn’t even know what genocide was. There was a great deal of discussion about what had happened. This was before the UN or the international community had decided that it was genocide.
I wanted to show how the country had been destroyed in those years, how people were trying to pick themselves up and find hope, even though they were truly devastated. At the same time, I’d always thought – indeed, I’d decided – that I didn’t want to make a film about this tragedy, because that was all anyone ever talked about, and we have so many other stories to tell, rich in nuances that can be captured and conveyed through cinema. So, if it wasn’t possible to make a film that excluded the genocide, I decided to keep it in the background anyway.
We also have many other stories to tell, rich in nuances that can be captured and told through cinema. I wanted to tell the story of a woman who wants to go to school, who wants to pursue a career. Today in Rwanda, women are at the forefront. We have 61 per cent female MPs in parliament. We have prominent female figures. I don’t think this happened overnight. It began thanks to women like Eva.

**Eva decides to leave her husband to continue her studies. Is that a realistic ending?**

Not really. Most women have remained trapped in their marriages. No one would have stopped a woman from leaving, but the problem was how society would have viewed and treated her afterwards. Most of the women I know who were abducted are still married and are around 50 years old. I wanted a happy ending for myself.

**How did you go about writing the screenplay?**

I wrote it in a very concise style. The film was made by a very small crew, with just a few actors and actresses, without traditional funding. This meant I had the freedom to write as I wanted, without having to spell everything out. I discussed the dialogue at length with the actresses.
Building on the screenplay, I worked with them to make the scenes involving sexuality realistic and believable. They are very young actresses and were deeply troubled by the story and by everything women have to endure to make a man happy. They understood that I wanted to recreate the chemistry of sisterhood between their characters.
The heart of the film is female friendship, a theme rarely explored because it’s often said that women stab each other in the back… For me, talking about female friendship – something society tries to ignore – is a political statement.

**The story is set in an area that seems uninhabited…**

The film was shot in north-western Rwanda; I wanted a sparsely populated place, both because in 1997 many people had not yet returned from exile and many homes had been destroyed, and because I wanted the house to be isolated to convey Eva’s sense of loneliness.

**Are you working on a new project?**

I’m writing a new screenplay. This time, however, I’d like to secure more substantial funding, whilst still remaining within the independent and African sphere. A low-budget film to be made quickly and free from (Western) influences.
