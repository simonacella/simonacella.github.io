_Souleymane's Story_ by Boris Lojkine, which won the Jury Prize and the Best Actor Award in the _Un certain regard_ section at Cannes 2024, is a breathless account of two days in the life of Souleymane, who has arrived in Paris from Guinea and is awaiting an interview with the OFPRA (the French Office for the Protection of Refugees and Stateless Persons), which will determine his fate by either accepting or rejecting his application for political asylum.

These are two crucial days, as Souleymane must obtain documents to submit to the Commission to prove the truth of his story. To get the documents, he has to pay Barry, a fellow Guinean who sells consultancy services and forged documents to the many Guineans seeking asylum. The only way to raise the money is to deliver food using the account of Emmanuel, a young man from Cameroon who works as a shop assistant in a supermarket and who takes an exorbitant cut from Souleymane’s meagre earnings. “You’ve got to work harder – even harder; you’re not earning enough,” Emmanuel presses him every time Souleymane rushes over to take the selfie that proves he is the actual owner of the account. But running, dashing through the streets of Paris, isn’t enough – and it’s dangerous. If even a single cog jams, the whole thing risks falling apart. It’s almost a contemporary version of the assembly line, without the social element of class struggle and solidarity. It is, in fact, a system where, on the face of it, everyone exploits and cheats everyone else. There are few spaces for solidarity, just small glimmers of humanity in a system where money and exploitation seem to prevail, even if only for the sake of mere survival.

Souleymane’s chain breaks when he is hit by a car. The impact appears to have no serious consequences, but the food bag is dented and the customer refuses the order, throwing Souleymane into a panic. Not only because of the lost earnings, but above all because of the risk that his account might be blocked. The pressure mounts; there is no money for Barry. Souleymane is worried because he hasn’t yet memorised the story he has to tell the Commission perfectly. He has to pretend that he left Guinea for political reasons, that he joined a party whose programme he supports, and that he was arrested and beaten. It’s a story he just can’t get his head round. Politics isn’t his thing; he’d like to change, but Barry reassures him and records audio messages for him containing useful information. Souleymane tries to pick up details from other Guineans too, but no one seems to have the time or inclination. Souleymane himself doesn’t even have time for a quick chat with his fellow countrymen; there’s no time for jokes or advice for the new arrivals. And the rush continues even after work, because the bus that will take him to the dormitory won’t wait. And yet again, every morning at dawn, he has to book a seat on that bus…

In this predominantly male world, which seems to revolve around survival and the exploitation of the weakest, there are only two female voices that we hear in sporadic telephone conversations between Souleymane and two women who have remained in Guinea and are awaiting his return. There is his ailing mother and Kadiatou, his young, lovesick fiancée who would be willing to make the sea crossing to join him… But it is Souleymane himself who has the task of bringing her back to reality, advising her to marry the engineer who has asked for her hand.

Souleymane’s journey comes to a halt when the day of his interview finally arrives. And here the film shifts to a different pace, where words are given time and space. Souleymane will be able to tell his story, but he will have to contend with a distorted system where lying seems to be the only way to gain protection.

The director wanted to make a film about delivery riders – people who are highly visible yet invisible because they have no documents. With the aim of building on a solid documentary foundation, he and his co-screenwriter met and interviewed many riders, discovering that almost all of them had, at some point in their lives, been obsessed with applying for asylum.
Political asylum is a complex issue, just as the application system is complex and distorted. In a choice that the director himself describes as political and anti-rhetorical, the protagonist had to be a man willing to lie in order to achieve his goal. With this premise in mind, the narrative was constructed with the thriller genre in mind rather than social commentary, confirming, amongst other things, how noir, in its various forms, has recently proved highly effective at portraying contemporary reality.

Abou Sangaré’s skill in playing Souleymane is undeniable; he succeeds in making the character believable. In real life, Abou Sangaré is a 24-year-old Guinean man who has been in France for eight years on a one-year residence permit obtained after numerous rejections. Abou works as a mechanic and, like his character, has no interest in politics. To reach France, he travelled via Libya, where he endured torture and abuse. To play the role, he had to learn the tricks, gestures and secrets of the delivery rider’s trade. Similarly, the director immersed himself in the Guinean community in Paris and in the workings of OFPRA, listening to interviews with asylum seekers and speaking with those who record the interviews, in order to gain a dual perspective and avoid falling into easy clichés.  The character of the woman conducting Souleymane’s interview – torn between the empathy she feels for the young man and the rules of the institution she works for – works because she is so realistic.

The challenge for Lojkine was to weave cinema into reality and bring as much reality as possible into fiction. To film Paris from a different perspective, slipping into the city with a small crew, navigating the real-life traffic to transform it into a soundtrack. Scenes featuring complex dialogue were deliberately set at the heart of city life: on the RER, in the midst of traffic, amongst the crowds.
The challenge has been met because _Souleymane’s Story_ succeeds in telling, through the dramatic tools of cinematic fiction, a contemporary story of migration from Africa to Europe. It is a precise story, rooted in reality yet emblematic of the situation faced by so many African migrants who have arrived in Europe hoping to build a new life. And the director hits the nail on the head when he observes that the drama is not centred, as in _Bicycle Thieves_, on the theft of a bicycle but on the difficulty of obtaining asylum. A bicycle can be bought again or, at a pinch, stolen; a residence permit cannot.
For this reason, among others, _Souleymane's Story_ gets to the heart of the problem.

Boris Lojkine, who previously directed [_Hope_](https://youtu.be/Fycw0Q0ahlo?si=5bCk8CDshd-Nqr7U) in 2014 – the story of a Nigerian girl and a Cameroonian boy undertaking the journey from Africa to Europe – and [_Camille_](https://youtu.be/LwhWDJGnw7Y?si=PfqBrowEeZ9FYYr9) from 2019 – a biopic about the photographer [Camille Lepage](https://www.camillelepage.org) who was killed in the Central African Republic – reaffirms his ability to use cinema to depict the contradictions of reality without resorting to rhetoric.
