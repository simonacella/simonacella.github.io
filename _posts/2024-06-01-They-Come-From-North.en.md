**Visions and narratives of a vanished city**  \
_Solo exhibition by Prasad Hettiarachchi_

Curated by Simona Cella

With _They come from North_, Kaiya Collective, in collaboration with Terzo Paesaggio, 5e6 and Fondazione Pianoterra, continues its exploration of artistic languages, territories and imaginaries explored within the artistic and workshop constellation that emerged from the production of Suranga Katugampala’s feature film _Still Here_. The residency, in line with Kaiya Collective’s philosophy, responds to a rebellious creative urgency, detached from the economic system and from neo-colonial mechanisms of exploitation of artists from the Global South. The entire project, conceived collectively, is based on a lean organisational structure and a sustainable budget, and was entirely self-funded in its early stages.

The artist’s work acts as a unifying magnet for a temporary constellation that brings together local organisations rooted in the area and active in urban and cultural regeneration with outsiders not yet recognised by institutions, funders or artistic and cultural circuits.

During his residency, Prasad Hettiarachchi will not only engage with artistic and cultural figures but will also interact with the Sri Lankan diaspora community, with whom he will organise a public art workshop.

A refined and socially engaged artist, Hettiarachchi has for years been observing and depicting the major social and urban transformations brought about by the Indian and Chinese neo-colonisation of Sri Lanka. The Visitors (They) come from a ‘North’ that no longer represents – or at least not exclusively – the former European colonisers or the United States, but rather new global powers such as China and India, interested in exploiting Sri Lanka’s natural and social resources, as the country serves as a central hub for the New Silk Road. Colombo is the driving force behind the artist’s entire body of work, and in particular the impact of recent urban and architectural development on the island’s capital. _They come from North_ features a selection of the artist’s work from 2019 up to her most recent pieces.

The presentation of the works in the exhibitions in Milan, Rome and Brescia employs various display techniques to highlight the multiple levels of interpretation and engagement with the works.

![They come from North](/assets/img/posts/theycomefromnorth2.jpg)

Firstly, there is the cinematic dimension, present in the form of transparencies in some works, which allow the viewer to play freely with the mechanism of projection whilst simultaneously evoking the gas5 warehouses in Colombo’s port and commercial district. The tables and lamps serve a dual purpose: they evoke the architect’s work whilst also inviting viewers to interpret the works as if they were large illustrated books or geographical maps. Central to the exhibition are two objects, _Saravita Box and Carro Armato (Katuru Muvahath)_, which operate metaphorically on multiple levels. The Saravita Box is a typical contemporary urban object used by street food vendors, who personalise it with decorations, lights and sounds to attract customers, transforming it into a performative object, whilst the _Carro Armato (Katuru Muvahath)_, also a performative object, is a mobile knife-sharpener that still roams the markets of Colombo today, offering to sharpen knives and blades.
The two works represent acquiescence to power and the oppression of the people, drawing on Gramscian thought, which is very close to the artist’s heart; he draws inspiration from the _Prison Notebooks_ to reflect on themes such as the alliance between workers and peasants, the relationship between intellectuals, class and the party, the State as a combination of political society and civil society in terms of domination and direction, and the development of a new philosophy of the proletariat.

This constellation of themes appears to stem from a crucial question that the artist brings to the fore through mythological figures reimagined in a contemporary context: why does the working class, even after Marx’s elucidation, continue to accept exploitation? What prevents it from finally establishing itself as a revolutionary subject? How can the oppressors, the ruling class, secure the consent of the oppressed, of the subalterns?
These are questions that touch upon the mechanisms of power, making Antonio Gramsci’s analyses and categories an important point of reference in the study of processes of colonial domination and decolonisation. Prasad Hettiarachchi thus explores Antonio Gramsci’s thought within the dual context of relations with the state and with the colonisers.

![They come from North](/assets/img/posts/theycomefromnorth3.jpg)

Power thus takes on new and complex forms, as in _The Queen_, a refined and elegant double portrait that superimposes the face of an enigmatic Chinese Empress onto that of Queen Elizabeth. Enveloped in a sumptuous cloak adorned with roses, thorns and birds of prey, she is the perfect embodiment of the ambiguity and dual nature of the new invasions.

In the portraits from the series _The Architect_, there are strong references to India, a country geographically close to and historically linked to Sri Lanka by a visceral relationship fraught with tensions and contradictions. The figure of Gandhi seems to come to life in the bodies of the architects, the new gurus of unbridled urban development. Leaning like ascetics against building site poles, their bodies and cloaks are embroidered with an intricate and anarchic architectural pattern in which ancient symbols – the swan, the lotus – now reduced to empty simulacra, navigate amongst motorways, pipes, wind turbines and menacing cranes. Round sunglasses shield the architects’ eyes, concealing their predatory and probing gaze. The architects’ concealed gaze is contrasted by the doubled eyes of the protagonists of the series _They Come from North_. Emerging from their heads are no longer sacks but transparent bubbles, which are nothing more than photographs of the Chinese building sites that have invaded the island. The transparency of the material makes possible the magic of a cinema of shadows and free projections.

![They come from North](/assets/img/posts/theycomefromnorth4.jpg)

In the drawings dedicated to Slave Island, a historic working-class neighbourhood falling victim to increasingly aggressive gentrification, white dominates and shapes an abstract landscape where, in a sophisticated interplay of symbolic references and complex layers, traditional architecture floats alongside menacing building sites, unsettling creatures and mutated bodies. Here, white—a colour traditionally associated with Buddhism and the concept of purity—undergoes a reversal of meaning, becoming the colour that absorbs and conceals the violence of a corrupt and brutal regime that frequently resorts to murder, kidnapping and cover-ups. And within this absorbing white, hooks from imposing cranes appear, like the talons of birds of prey, seizing mysterious sacks entangled in the inhabitants’ hair. The Sack, a central and recurring motif throughout the artist’s work, is a magic box containing memories and dreams, but also the belongings and clothes of a people increasingly forced to abandon their homes due to environmental disasters, wars or rampant gentrification. The artist does not show us the contents of these sacks; in the works _Our lives in the sack_ and Sack, they are also interpreted as papier-mâché sculptures – a material which, through the newspaper pages from which it is made, already embodies the chronicle of Sri Lanka. She does, however, paint their surfaces, drawing on the _Jataka Tales_, an immense treasure trove of stories and fables from Buddhist mythology that have, over the years, been plundered, censored and manipulated by nationalist ideologies. Hettiarachchi revisits this universe from a contemporary and non-religious perspective, depicting mythological figures who remain very much alive in the popular imagination. Central to this, for example, is the story of Patachara, a princess driven to madness by the grief of losing her family, who finds the path to Enlightenment through her encounter with the Buddha. It is an imaginary world where inhabitants forced into isolation by rampant urbanisation—which has dissolved social bonds—find refuge.

![They come from North](/assets/img/posts/theycomefromnorth5.jpg)

The series _Floating City_ depicts the dream of a new Colombo where, alongside luxurious skyscrapers – oases of well-being in a country gripped by inflation and the corruption of a political caste entrenched in power – the silhouette of Port City emerges: a Chinese city built on an artificial island of sand dredged from the ocean. A new form of luxury built on the labour of workers forced to toil in conditions of economic precariousness and insecurity to construct skyscrapers and wellness oases.

The viewer is invited to move fluidly across multiple levels of sight and sound: exhibition, art installation, geographical exploration, musical performance, expanded cinema. However one chooses to engage with it, Hettiarachchi’s vision – imbued with an Eastern and postcolonial interpretation of Marxist ideology and Gramscian thought – is at once art, a political manifesto and a critical reflection.

## Prasad Hettiarachchi
![Prasad Hettiarachchi](/assets/img/posts/Prasad-Hettiarachchi.jpg)

Hettiarachchi, originally from the Rajagiriya neighbourhood (Colombo), inherited his passion for art from his father, a woodcraftsman specialising in the construction of ritual lanterns. He trained in mural painting and archaeology at the Postgraduate Institute of Archaeology, University of Kelaniya, and after a brief stint in the world of advertising, in 2010 he decided to devote himself entirely to art. He initially collaborated with the Theertha Artists’ Collective before turning his attention to personal artistic exploration, which led him to experiment with various techniques and artistic languages. Over the years, his production of refined illustrations inspired by the art of miniatures has been complemented by mural art, woodcarving, sculpture, video art, design and Land Art.
From 2016 to 2022, in collaboration with the Central Cultural Fund (Matara District Project), he carried out major restoration work on paintings in ancient Buddhist temples. In 2022, he joined Kaiya Collective, a collective of multidisciplinary artists.
