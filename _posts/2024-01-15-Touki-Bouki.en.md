> A prophetic film. Beautiful, deeply moving and unexpected – it makes us question ourselves.

This is how Souleymane Cissé describes [_Touki Bouki_](https://www.raiplay.it/programmi/toukiboukiilviaggiodellaiena), Djibril Diop Mambety’s first feature film, a film shot in Dakar in 1973 that still shines with surprising relevance and clarity, especially in dark times such as those we are currently experiencing.
> Ecstatic, violent, entertaining, surprising

is how Martin Scorsese describes it in the foreword to the book [_Djibril Diop Mambety or the Journey of the Hyena_](https://www.amazon.it/ Djibril-cinematic-revolution-Senegalese-visionary/dp/8878923605), whilst also noting that the film was one of the first to be restored thanks to [_The World Cinema Project_](https://www.film-foundation.org/world-cinema?sortBy=title&sortOrder=1&page=6).

{% youtube "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdsANF5FlKY" %}

_Touki Bouki_ is a talismanic film that shields us from oblivion, rhetoric, boredom and the hypocrisy that seems to have invaded a certain strand of bourgeois European cinema, increasingly eager to ease its conscience by producing films about migration from Africa. A prime example is Matteo Garrone’s acclaimed but somewhat ambiguous _Io Capitano_.

Produced by Mambety, with a budget of 20 million CFA francs, shot in four weeks in Dakar and edited between Rome and Paris, _Touki Bouki_ is the natural evolution of his two previous films: the 1968 short film [_Contras City_](https://www.raiplay.it/programmi/contrascity) and the 1970 medium-length film [_Badou Boy_ ](https://www.raiplay.it/programmi/badouboy) from 1970, both of which were already remarkable for their style, tone and content.

{% youtube "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xaWmfqrQjU4" %}

At the heart of _Touki Bouki_ lies a strong desire for rebellion, which Mambety translates into precise cinematic choices. The story centres on the rejection of the ‘European dream’ which, even back then, was driving many young Senegalese to leave their country. A sick dream, in the director’s own words,  which creeps into the collective identity like a virus, causing a dangerous process of  cultural alienation.

> Yes, I wrote it and, in a sense, I lived it. This film is about Africans obsessed with Europe, Africans who believe that Europe is the gateway to Africa and that one must have been there to return home and gain respect. In a way, it’s about going to Europe for a ‘civilisation course’. For many, the way to get to Europe is by stowing away. I myself undertook a clandestine journey on a boat that took me as far as Marseille: but I was caught and sent back to Dakar. The film is, in a way, the story of many young people, and also reflects the revulsion I feel at this image of Europe that we are so determined to project onto Africa. An image created by Africans themselves, in fact. Those who, once they’ve returned, paint such a marvellous picture of Europe that you have only one desire: to go there, and you start to feel like a stranger in your own country. It is against this – of which I myself have been a victim – that I have rebelled in this film. Beyond the artistic exploration, I really wanted to do something useful, to try to dispel the illusions of my compatriots who have not left and remain obsessed with Europe. As for me, I have been cured of it.

No less important is the desire to reinvent cinema, which is an act of artistic creation but also of resistance against the colonialist ideology underpinning the production and distribution system of a certain kind of Western cinema.

> Just as my hero in *Touki Bouki* refused, whilst the ship was still there, to go to Europe, so too have I rejected the mechanism of vision upon which Western cinema has relied to win over the minds of the Senegalese public.

These words encapsulate the poetics and politics underpinning the director’s entire body of work, as he seeks a (cinematic) synthesis that captures the complexity of post-independence Senegalese culture by reshaping, in new forms, the dialogical relationships between tradition and modernity, and between African and Western culture – themes that have inspired much of African cinema.

The story told in _Touki Bouki_ is seemingly simple as well as tragically topical.
Mory, a solitary motorcyclist, and Anta, a rebellious student, are in love and dream of a bright future in Paris. To scrape together the money needed for the journey, they roam Dakar, devising scams and thefts in a constant game of disguises and ironic performances. When it comes time to board the ship, however, Mory runs away, leaving Anta to set off alone.

![Mory and Anta](/assets/img/posts/toukiboukitesto.jpg)

Mambety exploits the formal and stylistic potential of cinematic language to the full, distilling powerful, hypnotic, imperfect images and leaving it to the viewer to piece together the story and decipher its complex symbolic layers.

The story of Anta and Mory draws on deep roots that probe a chaotic Dakar, steeped in memory and mystery. Dakar is a place of modernity, power and wealth. Here, capitalism has erected its temples and imposed its rituals.  The abattoir that sacrifices not only the zebu but also the slow rhythm of pastoral life. The port, with its endless containers and ships ferrying aid workers and neo-colonialists back and forth. The university, churning out students fresh from the struggles of ’68 yet already conformist and bourgeois, just like most of the post-independence ruling class.

Dakar is a place of illusions. Lapped by the ocean,  which here appears in all its power as both Eros and  Thanatos,  it is the perfect vantage point from which to dream of escape.
Dakar is the dual city, which already contains within itself the city of one’s dreams. The coveted Eldorado, embodied by the voice of Josephine Baker (‘Paris Paris Paris, c’est sur la Terre un coin de paradis’) and by the siren of the ship Ancerville, Paris appears as a watermark throughout the film, whilst even on the poster an upside-down Eiffel Tower highlights the bitter irony of a dream turned on its head. Surrounded by a brousse dotted with enormous baobabs, Dakar is also a liminal, hybrid space that evokes a pastoral past but also a present where amulets can alter one’s destiny.  And, with a mocking nod to the character of Tarzan, Mambety also finds room for a ‘white savage’ who lives in the trees and who will burn to death, dazzled (he too) by modernity.

As they wander, Mory and Anta play with space and identities, breaking down real and imaginary boundaries and charting the anarchic, fiery course of a rite-of-passage journey that will end in a parting.
Mory rides fearlessly on a motorbike decorated with zebu horns and the double cross of the Dogon’s Kanaga mask. We sense that he has a past as a shepherd-boy and bear witness to a rite-of-passage journey that will take him back to his roots, perhaps allowing him to gain greater mastery over the world. We see him travelling the streets of Dakar whilst a continuous stream of visual and auditory consciousness presents us with the kaleidoscopic, swirling image of his intimate and fragmented mystical journey.
A journey that encompasses sacrifice as the ultimate ritual act.
At his side, Anta embodies the new post-independence woman, the perfect icon of a generation that manages to blend the sacred with the profane, tradition with modernity, and the masculine with the feminine, and which remains steadfast in its pursuit of freedom even at the cost of losing love.

![Anta](/assets/img/posts/Antatesto.jpg)

Premiered at the famous Théâtre Sorano in Dakar, _Touki Bouki_ was booed and harshly criticised by audiences and critics alike, who were completely unprepared for such an extremely experimental, provocative and political film.
Awarded at Cannes by the Quinzaine des Réalisateurs and at the Moscow International Film Festival, it is now considered a masterpiece of post-colonial cinematic modernism and an essential reference point for many African directors (and others) seeking an innovative cinematic language.
From the outset, critics spoke of a film influenced by the Nouvelle Vague and Cinema Novo. It is certainly avant-garde cinema, but it is important not to overlook its militant aspect, which fiercely criticises not only the colonialism that violently imposed alien systems such as urbanisation, industrialisation, capitalism and Catholicism, but also the bitter betrayal of independence at the hands of a ‘Poet President’ who skilfully concealed an overly pro-French mindset and a tendency towards an iron fist and censorship.
_Touki Bouki_ is a deeply anti-capitalist film, just as _Hyènes_ would be twenty years later.
