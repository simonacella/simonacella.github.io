## Golden Bear for *Dahomey*, the documentary on artefacts returned to Benin

![Film crew of *Dahomey* at the Berlinale](/assets/img/posts/dahomey2.jpg)

Mati Diop won the Golden Bear at the 74th Berlinale for _Dahomey_, a documentary dedicated to the return of 26 artefacts looted in 1892 from the Kingdom of Dahomey (now Benin) by French colonial troops led by General Dodds and held until 2021 at the Musée de l’Homme, now the Musée du quai Branly in Paris.

Presented with the award by Lupita Nyong’o (Chair of a jury comprising Brady Corbet, Ann Hui, Christian Petzold, Albert Serra, Jasmine Trinca and Oksana Zabuzhko), the French director of Senegalese origin accepted the award by proudly rejecting historical amnesia as a method (of neo-colonial domination) and declaring solidarity with Palestine and the Senegalese people, who have been fighting for democracy and justice for months.

## A thorny issue

[The issue of restitution](https://www.nigrizia.it/notizia/patrimonio-artistico-africano-continente-saccheggiato) is complex and has emerged forcefully in the cultural debate, involving above all France and England, whose Louvre and British Museum house thousands of artefacts stolen during colonial rule. ‘Within five years, we must be in a position to facilitate the return of African heritage to Africa. It is a priority,’ said Emmanuel Macron on 28 November 2017 in a speech at the University of Ouagadougou, in Burkina Faso; but as early as 2016, the President of Benin, Patrice Talon, had been calling for the restitution of the royal treasure.

{% youtube "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ogyFVfd4IUM" %}

The theme of return has always been present in Mati Diop’s films. From [Milles Soleils](https://vimeo.com/89437102), where she engages with the cult film _Touki Bouki_ and her African roots (it was at the Quai Branly that the director paid tribute to Mambety’s cinema at a [talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ua3z-IQDj6M) at the Université Populaire) to the figure of the revenant in [Atlantiques](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cv3BWPfBwYc).

The director speaks of a short-circuit between restitution, revenge, return and reparation that led her to want to make the documentary. When the selection of the 26 works that were to be returned to [Benin](https://www.nigrizia.it/ news/benin-crossroads-of-jihadist-pressure), Mati Diop set about organising the shoot, involving the government of the African country in the film and setting up a Senegalese production company to co-produce the film with France.

## Between magical realism and rigorous storytelling

The documentary, described by the director herself as belonging to the fantasy genre, experiments with a style that blends multiple approaches. Whilst the preparation of the artworks for the journey is filmed with the rigour of observational documentary, magical realism is used to bring the artworks to life, particularly the statue of King Gizo, which speaks in the Fon language through the words and voice of the Haitian poet Makenzy Orce. A stream of consciousness that, through the darkness of the containers in which the King is confined, conveys his thoughts and existential concerns, as well as his disappointment at having been labelled and identified by a number, and his disorientation upon emerging from the realm of night and arriving in a country very different from the one he had dreamt of.

This historical moment thus also takes on a mythical dimension; yet this is not reduced to blind ideological celebration, but rather, with intellectual honesty, acknowledges King Gizo’s share of responsibility in the slave trade of the mid-19th century. Another important voice is present: that of Calixte Biah, curator of the Oudiah History Museum, who accompanies the shipment from Paris to Cotonou. His reverence for the treasure and his detailed account of the condition and origin of the works provide the necessary historical and artistic context.

## The debate widens

In Benin, the camera lingers and explores at length – almost as if it were an architectural essay – the space within the presidential palace housing the artefacts, before becoming a neutral tool for recording the heated debate amongst students at the University of Abomey-Calavi on pressing issues such as cultural heritage, colonial policy, the real motivations behind restitution, the consequences of the long absence of the royal treasures for the country’s culture, which for years had to contend with an enormous void and an irreplaceable loss. In this way, Mati Diop liberates the debate on restitution, bringing it out of the political and academic circles where it had been confined.

«For me, the challenge was to find a way to create a space for free expression on a subject that belongs entirely to those directly involved,’ said the director. “On the day of filming, I chose to broadcast the debate on the campus radio station to create more tension and a sense of urgency amongst the speakers, who knew they were being listened to by a wider audience. Quite apart from the filming, it was only right that the debate should be broadcast and shared with as many people as possible.»

The most direct cinematic reference to _Dahomey_ is undoubtedly [Les statues meurent aussi](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_statues_meurent_aussi) (1953) by Alain Resnais and Chris Marker, a reflection on African art and colonialism. But Sokurov’s _Francofonia_, about the works of Ancient Greece plundered by Napoleon, also springs to mind. _Dahomey_ is not, however, a nostalgic film but a deeply contemporary one. Indeed, it does not deny the contradictions that may lie behind cultural initiatives such as those involving restitution. The crowds of visitors flocking to see the exhibited works highlight the risk that Benin’s national treasures might become like the mini Eiffel Towers seen at the start of the film: tourist trinkets for visitors who are unaware of the historical and cultural significance of the works in front of which they are taking selfies.
