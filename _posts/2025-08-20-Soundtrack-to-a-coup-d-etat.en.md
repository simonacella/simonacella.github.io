Max Roach’s drumming sets the rhythm for the words of a promise that appear as text on a black screen.

> On Friday, our women will go to the United Nations.
>
> We will rise up and stand our ground.
>
> See you soon, sister. Meet at the UN at 8.30 am on Friday.

Abbey Lincoln appears on screen. Her singing is accompanied by the text

> Our brother Lumumba has been killed in the Congo.

A duet by Max Roach and Abbey Lincoln, and the promise by a group of women to meet at the UN to protest against the killing of Patrice Lumumba.
Thus begins Johan Grimonprez’s compelling documentary, which recounts, to the rhythm of jazz, the behind-the-scenes story of Lumumba’s assassination. He was killed in Katanga on 16 January 1961 at the hands of Mobutu, acting on behalf of the CIA and the Belgians, with the complicity of the UN.

![Lumumba](/assets/img/posts/lumumba.jpg)

The film covers the period from 1955 to 1965 and is as gripping as a spy novel might be when read whilst listening to a compilation of the finest African-American jazz musicians: Louis Armstrong, Dizzy Gillespie, Duke Ellington, Thelonious Monk, Nina Simone, Miles Davis, John Coltrane…

At the heart of the story lies the plot hatched by Belgium, which – having been forced to grant independence to the Congo – orchestrated a ‘divide and rule’ strategy, the most obvious result of which was the secession of the province of Katanga, a region home to the country’s most valuable mineral deposits.
A plot supported by the United States, determined to maintain control over the Congo’s uranium (which was used to build the atomic bomb), with the complicity of the United Nations, which was entirely subservient to American interests.

The historical context is that of the Cold War and the struggle against colonialism. The world is divided into three major blocs: the Capitalists, the Communists and the Non-Aligned. The Congo, rich in raw materials, is at the centre of tensions that extend beyond its geographical borders: the US–USSR rivalry, the struggle for African independence, and the emergence of the Afro-Asian bloc, which, thanks to the birth of 16 independent African nations, has disrupted the internal balance of power within the UN.

To delve into the dark side of Belgium’s colonial past – his own country of origin – the director uses audio memoirs, narrated excerpts from political novels, speeches, interviews, footage of performances, amateur films, official texts, historical footage, official documents and newsreel footage from the period.
The narrative is non-linear and reflects both the complexity of one of the most obscure conundrums of post-independence Africa and the ideological fervour of that period.

The soundtrack to a coup d’état that led to Lumumba’s assassination is a complex polyphony set to a jazz rhythm – a jazz that not only sets the pace of the narrative but also reminds us that conspiracies are more sophisticated than we might realise from a cursory glance at history.

> If Africa is shaped like a revolver, then the Congo is its bullet (Franz Fanon)

At its core lie the testimonies and ideas of Lumumba, Andrée Blouin, In Koli Jean Bofane and Malcolm X. There is a chorus (Eisenhower, Belgium, the UN, the CIA, the mercenaries, Khrushchev, Nkrumah, Nasser, Malcolm X, Fidel Castro, the leaders of the non-aligned Afro-Asian countries, Miriam Makeba, a Lumumbist guerrilla fighter).

And then there are the jazz musicians. Grimonprez takes up the theory that the State Department used Armstrong and other black musicians to divert attention from Lumumba’s assassination, sending them on tour to African countries as goodwill ambassadors. “ ‘The visits by these jazz ambassadors almost always heralded a coup d’état,’ says the director, also recalling Dizzy Gillespie’s trip to Syria in 1956 and Duke Ellington’s concert in Iraq in 1963, which, in fact, coincided with that year’s coup d’état. “And I found it very strange that the State Department would send jazz musicians to cover up plans and policies of which not even the musicians themselves were aware. There was literally a moment when Louis Armstrong had dinner with Larry Devlin, the head of the CIA in Leopoldville, and Armstrong didn’t have the faintest idea!”

In those years, musicians such as Louis Armstrong, Dizzy Gillespie, Duke Ellington, Sarah Vaughan, Quincy Jones and Nina Simone were effectively persuaded to tour Africa and Asia as part of cultural awareness initiatives – activities that later turned out to be CIA-backed public relations operations.
“America’s weapon was a blue note in a minor key,” read one report.
A discordant note that confronted African-American musicians with the contradiction of representing a country where racial segregation was still in force.

Grimonprez uses cinema to its fullest potential not only to tell a story but to recreate a confusion akin to the distraction created by hidden power.
The audiovisual material superimposes a voice telling us one thing onto an image showing another, whilst someone plays a jazz piece and written text appears. A vortex that draws the viewer in with a hypnotic effect, reinforced by the repetition of images (parachutes floating in the sky like enormous jellyfish, an elephant suspended in mid-air being thrown into a ship’s container, a giant octopus, a submarine, children playing) that open up dreamlike and symbolic passages, adding a further layer of depth to the already intense collective memoir.

![Elephant transported in a cargo ship](/assets/img/posts/elefante.jpg)

One must see, read and listen repeatedly to extrapolate and understand the various levels of this Congolese narrative, which remains tragically relevant today. It is a vision that demands the utmost attention but which sheds light on vast grey areas of a history that still directly involves us today, not only as consumers of goods (as the Apple or Tesla adverts remind us) but also as consumers of culture and art.
Perhaps not everyone is aware that the then president of New York’s MoMA, William Burden, was not only a shareholder in the Katanga mining industry but also the US ambassador to Belgium and a CIA secret agent. And, it goes without saying, he too considered it necessary to eliminate Lumumba, both politically and physically.

> MoMA’s board of directors was like a CIA yearbook

The intricate link between contemporary art and colonialism is one that has been little explored. Who knows, perhaps the much-heralded restitution of works of art to African countries (Mati Diop’s [_Dahomey_](https://simonacella.github.io/Dahomey.html)) serves to make us forget the dark soul of the white cubes? ( [ _White Cube_](https://youtu.be/HRcaiNavd7U?si=9__ZLRDXcVs6Owbr) by Renzo Martens ).

Fortunately, luminous fragments also emerge from this maelstrom, torn from the shadows of a memory that is all too often concealed, severed or manipulated. A light that echoes Lumumba’s words on Independence Day:
> We shall make the Congo a centre that will shine its light across the whole of Africa.
The Hotel Theresa in Harlem, where activists and leaders from non-aligned nations would go to listen to Lumumba’s speeches and witness the meeting between Castro and Khrushchev. Between Castro and Malcolm X. A Bandung Conference on American soil, standing in contrast to the UN General Assembly, that sad and grey crime scene.

![hotel teresa](/assets/img/posts/hotelteresa.jpg)
<small>Fidel Castro and Malcolm X at the Hotel Theresa, Harlem, September 1960</small>

Andrée Blouin, sent from Guinea to the Congo to organise a women’s movement in support of the Mouvement National Congolais’ election campaign, went on to become Lumumba’s speechwriter and head of protocol. Portrayed by the Belgian secret services as a communist and a courtesan to African leaders, and expelled from the Congo a few days before independence, she managed to smuggle to safety—hidden in her chignon—the document proving Lumumba’s legitimacy (rather than that of Joseph Kasa-Vubu, the Belgian candidate) to lead the government.

![andree](/assets/img/posts/soundtrack-copertina.jpg)

In [Congo Inc. Bismarck’s Testament](https://66thand2nd.com/products/congo-inc), Koli Jean Bofane reminds us how the Congo, a constantly evolving algorithm between Washington, London, Brussels and Kigali, served as the global supplier of raw materials essential to global wars: rubber for the First World War, uranium for the atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and copper for the bullets in Vietnam. Not to mention the raw materials needed to take the war into space.

The testimony of Corron Cruise O’Brien, then head of the UN Contingent in Katanga, on the genocide of the Balubakat at the hands of mercenaries from Belgium, France, Germany and South Africa. A rare account of how the powers that be use the UN to influence international politics.
I shall conclude by quoting a key sequence that illustrates better than any treatise the power of the voice of New Africa in contrast to the arrogance and stupidity of old Europe, clinging to a colonial power
now in a state of decay.

On 29 June 1960, King Baudouin arrived in Leopoldville for the proclamation of the Congo’s independence, unwittingly becoming the protagonist of a ridiculous vanity fair in two acts.
The first act sees him strutting pompously in a royal procession, so full of himself that he fails to notice that a distinguished man in a suit and tie, Ambroise Boimbo, reaches out as the king’s car passes and steals the ceremonial sword.

![The theft of the sword ](/assets/img/posts/furto-spada.jpg)

The second act sees him deliver a shameful speech in which he attributes the Congo’s independence to the genius of Leopold II and the perseverance of Belgium.

> How will independence arrive?
> By car?
> By ship?
> Or by plane?
>
> _Une saison au Congo_ by Aimé Césaire

It is Lumumba’s words, in an unscheduled speech, that set the record straight, recalling Belgium’s true role and vindicating his people’s struggle of “tears, fire and blood”.

> We are proud, to the very depths of our souls, to have waged a struggle that was one of tears, blood and fire, for it was a noble, just and necessary struggle to
> put an end to the humiliating slavery that was forcibly imposed upon us.
> This has been our lot throughout eighty years of colonial rule, and our wounds are too fresh and painful to be erased from our memory. How could we ever forget,
> we who know the gruelling labour that prevents us from satisfying our hunger, from clothing ourselves and living with dignity, and from educating our children as is required?
>
> From Lumumba’s speech during the independence ceremony, 30 June 1960

Watch the film. Read the closing credits carefully. Listen to the music.
