Screened in the official competition at the 1992 Cannes Film Festival, _Hyènes_ marks Mambety’s troubled return to cinema. Whilst [_Touki Bouki_](/en/Touki-Bouki.html) was born out of an urgent desire to revolutionise cinema by recounting an entire generation’s longing for the ‘Elsewhere’, _Hyènes_ stems from an obsession with finding Anta, «the girl who had had the courage to leave Africa and cross the Atlantic alone».

Speculating on the young woman’s possible fate, Mambety constructs a new character by superimposing two powerful female figures. The first is a prostitute from Dakar, known to everyone as Linguere Ramatou, who one day disappears as mysteriously as she had appeared. Mambety invents a legendary past for her, imagining her as a child in her village, where one night the villagers set fire to her family’s hut, suspecting them of witchcraft. The remains of little Linguere are not found alongside those of the rest of her family, and from that moment on the entire village lives in terror of Linguere’s return. The character remained ‘dormant’ in the director’s imagination for years until, whilst watching Bernard Wicki’s film [_La Rancune_](https://youtu.be/0Y681DYBXec?si=6WLP_8VY7Nx-xyYX) (1964), adapted from Friedrich Dürrenmatt’s _The Visit, the image of Ingrid Bergman—who plays Claire Zachanassian in the film—brought her back to the director’s mind. Mambety read the Swiss playwright’s text and decided to adapt it for the screen.

Thus was born _Hyenes_, the story of Linguere Ramatou’s return to Colobane: now a billionaire, she is prepared to shower the town with gold on condition that Draman Drameh, the man who betrayed her thirty years earlier, is sentenced to death. Colobane, an urban metaphor embodying bankruptcy, economic disaster and despair, is a dystopian fusion of the town of Güllen described by Dürrenmatt and an anonymous town in the Sahel. The setting is that of a Western. In Colobane there is a railway station, an inn, the general store/saloon, the town hall, the police station and the church, to which are added two spaces that evoke the African context: the Hyenas’ Den and the Elephant Graveyard. Its boundaries are marked by the sea and the boundless, ochre-yellow expanse of the desert and the savannah. A space traversed by gendarmes wielding wooden rifles, who escort on horseback the arrival of lorries laden with luxury goods. It is in this dusty little town that, as in a Greek tragedy, Linguere Ramatou – a heroine of mythical and symbolic significance – and Draman Drameh, a man alone in the face of his destiny, come face to face.

Linguere Ramatou, played by the non-professional actress Ami Diakhate, is a cyborg with gold prostheses who bears on her body the marks of a cruel exile. As the director explains, ‘Linguere’ means ‘Sole Queen’ in the Wolof language, whilst ‘Ramatou’ is the red bird from a legend of Pharaonic Black Egypt. A sacred bird that cannot be killed with impunity: it is, in fact, the soul of the dead. Ramatou arrives in Colobane accompanied by three Amazons, a Japanese bodyguard, the former judge of Colobane and two false witnesses, whom she has castrated and reduced to eunuch slaves. She defines herself through two images: money and hell; and whilst asserting that the present belongs to her, her constant references to the past bind her to the concept of memory. She is a goddess of vengeance who echoes Pasolini’s [_Medea_](https://youtu.be/HN-KHdL1Hs0?si=LTDZdY0_wbV9ZdLv) with her sacrifices, her pain at betrayed love and her premonitory dream of vengeance. But she is, above all, the embodiment of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, because «she turns up with many billions and asks an extremely poor people to make a sacrifice».

Draman Drameh, played by the non-professional actor Mansour Diouf, is a solitary hero. Initially tasked with saving Colobane, by the second act he is a man on the run, transformed against his will into a scapegoat. Only at the end does he become a tragic hero, choosing – as a clear-eyed witness to society’s moral disintegration – to atone for a long-ago transgression in order to save his own city. It is through Draman that we catch a glimpse of the desire to continue Fred Zinnemann’s [_High Noon_](https://youtu.be/vFRm6xPDLFc?si=I_IimQAWMXFQlMcz), ‘the western of my childhood’, the film that sparked Mambety’s desire to make films. A drama of a man confronting loneliness, fear and death, ‘a study of fear against the backdrop of a city’s cowardice’, _High Noon_ seems to tell the story of _Hyenes_ from Draman Drameh’s perspective. Draman, who knows he must die, and whose lucid awareness we shall rediscover years later in the character of Satché, the protagonist of [_Tey_](https://youtu.be/bHEwqSlzNOc?si=8vaTGALgzBKcVZkH) (2012) by Alain Gomis.

The confrontation between Linguere and Draman takes place through the mediation of the inhabitants of Colobane who, lured by the mirage of money, will transform into a ravenous horde that will ‘devour’ Draman, sacrificing him on the altar of the god of money. It is through the transformation of the inhabitants of Colobane that Mambety offers a ruthless analysis of an Africa subjugated to neo-colonialism.

_Hyenes_ superimposes the Western genre onto a theatrical text (which already contains elements of Greek theatre) and, as in the most classic of genres, the legal drama, uses the ritual of the trial as a central and highly symbolic narrative device. In 2006, Abderrahmane Sissako, with [_Bamako_](https://youtu.be/MOt0IlNBll0?si=dQ5H4vMBUPuvpw_J), would go on to depict another informal tribunal, within which representatives of African civil society put the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund on trial.

_Hyènes_ is a postcolonial tropical western, a dystopian film that offers a lucid and bitter reflection on exile and memory, on forgiveness and revenge, on the power of money and the loss of identity, and on loneliness.

Another film springs to mind. It is [_Guelwaar_](https://dai.ly/x8hrknv) by Sembene Ousmane, released in 1993. It tells the story of the murder of a leading advocate for an uncorrupted and free Africa. Due to an administrative error, his body – despite him being Catholic – was buried in a Muslim cemetery. The dispute that erupts between the two social groups – highlighting the still considerable importance of ritual – allows the director to denounce an Africa that reveres the gifts of foreign benefactors, at the risk of being reduced to a state of beggary.
The two masters of Senegalese cinema thus find themselves united in a shared vision of cinema: poetic cinema; political cinema.
