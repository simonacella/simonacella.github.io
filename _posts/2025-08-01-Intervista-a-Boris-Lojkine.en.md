**Interview with Boris Lojkine**

I met Boris Lojkine in Paris at the Bistrot Au fil du vin in the 10th arrondissement a few days before New Year’s Eve. Whilst writing my review of [_The Story of Souleymane_](/en/La-storia-di-Souleymane.html), I had come across his two previous films, _Hope_ (2014) and _Camille_ (2017), which, together with his latest film, form an ‘African’ trilogy. These three films share a documentary-style approach and a precise dramatic structure in which suspense, drama and dialogue respond perfectly to the demands of cinematic fiction without betraying reality or allowing the narrative to fall into those traps (excessive rhetoric, implausible scripts or television-style reconstructions) that often make it impossible for a white, middle-class director (as Boris describes himself) to tell stories of migration from Africa.

This lengthy Parisian conversation traces the journey of a director who, having completed a thesis in philosophy and spent time in Vietnam – where he made two documentaries on the Vietcong’s inability to come to terms with the grief of war – set off in 2008 on a journey to the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Kinshasa, Kisangani, but also the rainforest and encounters with the Pygmies and the Mai-Mai rebels.
His plans to make documentaries clashed not only with the country’s difficulties but also with his desire to create an epic feature film about the journey of African migrants heading for Europe. An epic subject, impossible to ignore, declares Lojkine, just as the Vietnam War was for a certain generation of American filmmakers.

![Hope](/assets/img/posts/hopetesto.jpg)

With the mindset of a documentary-maker but a firm conviction to make a feature-length fiction film, Lojkine researched the subject by reading books, reports, NGO documents and migrants’ testimonies. Different stories but with common elements that serve as the basis for writing a screenplay. A process that took two years but, as often happens, was completely called into question by field research. “After a trip to Morocco and Algeria, everything changed,” he admits. In Morocco, in fact, he discovered the world of the ghettos. “Cameroon. Nigeria. Congo. Every country has a ghetto. Every ghetto has its own laws. Its own government, its own president. ” He realises that it is essential to understand the laws and rules of the ghettos and begins to explore an underground world – unknown and difficult to penetrate. “The Nigerian leaders are the most powerful and violent. They run the prostitution trade. They follow military hierarchies and run veritable prisons.” He discovers that the reality for women is profoundly different. Most are forced into prostitution. “There are many sex workers in Rabat and Casablanca. And it’s very difficult to manage to speak to them freely.”

“The story thus underwent a profound transformation. At first, I wanted to tell the story of a migrant trying to reach Europe. I realised, however, that a dramatic arc was missing. The obstacles the protagonist would have faced would have been the same as those faced by everyone undertaking the journey. I decided I would tell the story of a couple’s journey. And the central question of the narrative would be: ‘Will the couple stay together until the end?’ It wasn’t, however, about telling a romantic love story; talking about love in such a violent world would have seemed forced, an artifice. And so the story of Léonard, from Cameroon, and Hope, from Nigeria, takes shape: they meet by chance whilst crossing the desert on the border between Morocco and Algeria. She needs a protector; he doesn’t have the heart to abandon her. Breaking the strict rules of the ghetto, they will try to cling to one another, exploiting each other in the process. At the start of their relationship, Léonard and Hope talk only of money and their need to get to Europe; it is only with time that they discover the possibility of imagining a romantic relationship.

“In Morocco, the power of the Nigerian community was very clear. I decided it would be interesting to pair a Nigerian girl with a Cameroonian character, precisely because of the contrast between the two communities.” The cast consists entirely of non-professional actors who come from the ghettos and know their dynamics very well. “I found all the actors in Morocco. During auditions, people are more inclined to speak.” In this way, social rules and mechanisms are revealed to the director, who develops his narrative. During the five months spent in Morocco, the director reconstructs the ghettos, aided by the actors and extras. With a small crew, he follows the rhythms of fictional cinema. There is just one documentary-style scene: the one in the Gourougou forest where migrants recount the attack on the barrier protecting the Spanish enclave of Melilla. It was important that, at a certain point in the film, fiction should encounter an indisputable reality to remind us that all this is not just cinema.

![Camille](/assets/img/posts/camilletesto.jpg)

_Hope_ is being screened at Cannes’ Critics’ Week, but Lojkine’s African adventure continues with _Camille_, a biopic about Camille Lepage, a young French photojournalist who was killed in the Central African Republic in 2014, after spending two years documenting the civil war between the Seleka and Anti-Balaka factions. “Camille is my alter ego. Like me, she explored the deeper meaning of creating images.” The working method is the same as in _Hope_. “I met everyone who knew her: family members, friends, work colleagues. I tried to understand the rules of the trade, what editing involves, and what a newspaper expects from a photojournalist. In 2017, I went to Bangui, in the Central African Republic, where I worked as a trainer alongside Daniele Incalcaterra on film-making workshops organised by Atelier Varan. Two and three months were spent teaching the basic techniques of filmmaking. In two months, the students produce a film. We discuss the footage together. It was a highly formative experience that led to the creation of 10 short films about the country. An experience that really opened my eyes. Many of my students from the Atelier collaborated with me on the film about Camille.

From those very same ‘Ateliers’, as we recall, came Rafiki Fariola, director of [_Nos étudiants_](https://youtu.be/-qVHdb0FZ0Q?si=zrSqd2w8V2VQS9vz), presented at the 2022 Berlinale and produced by Lojkine himself alongside Daniele Incalcaterra.
For _The Story of Souleymane_ too, documentary material formed the basis for constructing a precise narrative structure. Extensive interviews with delivery riders and asylum seekers, two years of writing and an open casting call. And above all, a flexible crew that adapts to the needs of the various sets. The result is a breathless account of two days in the life of Souleymane, who has arrived in Paris from Guinea and is awaiting the interview that will determine his fate by accepting or rejecting his application for political asylum.
When we ask him about Souleymane’s fate, he smiles and tells us that it is the only question he will never answer.
